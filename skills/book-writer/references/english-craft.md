# English Craft Notes

For manuscripts in English. The standard is written in Bengali, but its rules are about writing, not about the language, so all of it applies to English work. The one exception is §10 (Bengali language and typography), which this file replaces.

## 1. House defaults

- **Spelling:** follow the user's or the manuscript's variety. If there is none, use British spelling, the convention in Bangladeshi education and publishing. Keep -ise/-ize and similar choices consistent.
- **Quotes:** double curly quotes for dialogue, with single quotes nested inside them. Keep to one convention.
- **Numbers:** in fiction, spell out zero to one hundred and round numbers. In nonfiction, choose a rule (spell out one to nine, or one to one hundred) and keep it. Use numerals with currency, percentages and dates.
- **Bangladeshi money and scale:** pick one currency style (Tk 25,000 or BDT 25,000) and keep it. For local readers, lakh and crore are natural. For international readers, use thousands and millions. Don't mix the two systems.
- **Bengali words in English prose:** don't italicize everyday words (rickshaw, biryani, sari, Eid), and don't gloss what the context makes clear. Choose one transliteration for each word and name (Pohela Boishakh, Ammu, Apa) and keep it.

## 2. Punctuation

- No em dash. §21.10 applies to English too. Don't substitute a spaced en dash, a double hyphen, or a spaced hyphen. Use a comma, colon, semicolon, parentheses, or two sentences.
- Use the en dash only for ranges (pages 10–12, 2019–2024).
- Choose whether to use the serial comma and keep to it.
- Exclamation marks are rare and belong mostly in dialogue. Use ellipses for trailing speech, not for mood.

## 3. Patterns that read as generated

None of these is wrong once. Together they are the fingerprint of unedited model prose, and `scripts/check_manuscript.py` flags many of them.

- **Vocabulary:** delve, tapestry, testament to, realm, navigate (for anything abstract), myriad, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, bustling, nestled, palpable, intricate, multifaceted, seamless, robust, leverage, foster, underscore, embark, unlock, journey (as metaphor), landscape (abstract), elevate, resonate.
- **Constructions:**
  - "It's not X, it's Y."; "not just X, but Y"; "X isn't about Y. It's about Z."
  - A rule-of-three list in every paragraph.
  - A rhetorical question followed by its answer.
  - "Here's the thing:"
  - Runs of dramatic fragments ("Silence. Then footsteps. Then nothing.").
  - A moral as the last line ("And maybe that's the point.").
  - "Little did she know".
- **Fiction tics:**
  - Filter verbs (she felt, saw, heard, noticed, realized, wondered) that put a pane of glass between reader and event.
  - Naming an emotion right after showing it.
  - Default body choreography: eyes widening, breath hitching, heart pounding, jaw clenching.
  - "A mix of X and Y".
  - Smiles that don't reach the eyes.
  - Adverbs on dialogue tags ("said softly").
- **Nonfiction tics:**
  - Openings like "In today's fast-paced world".
  - "It's important to note".
  - Signposting ("In this chapter, we will explore").
  - A summary at the end of every section.
  - "Let's dive in".
  - Hype and hedging in the same paragraph.

## 4. Dialogue

- Use "said" as the default tag, plus action beats. Start a new paragraph for each speaker. People speak in contractions.
- Bangladeshi characters speaking English sound like individuals, not a caricature. Bengali words (Abba, Ammu, bhai, apa, ji) appear only where that character would naturally use them, spelled consistently.

## 5. Nonfiction voice

- Addressing the reader as "you" is fine. Pressure phrasing is not (§13.2): "you must", "starting today", "your life will change".
- Label illustrative examples as hypothetical ("Say you earn Tk 30,000 a month…"). Give statistics only with a verifiable source. Otherwise phrase the point qualitatively, or put a verification note outside the manuscript (§9).
