# Genre Craft

§2.2, §7 and §15 of the standard set the principles. This file adds craft specifics for the categories the platform publishes most: Mystery, Thriller, Horror, Romance, Adventure and Religious, plus children's and YA books. Read each section as a list of what commonly goes wrong, not as a formula. §7.1 rules out forcing every story into one structure.

## Mystery

- **Fair play.** Every clue the solution depends on appears on the page before the reveal, in a form an attentive reader could notice (§7.8). Plant the decisive clue early and in plain sight. Ideally it does double duty, seeming to mean something else.
- The detective solves the case by reasoning from evidence the reader has seen. Luck, a sudden confession, or information kept from the reader doesn't count.
- Each suspect has a motive, an opportunity, and a reason to lie about something, not necessarily the crime. Each red herring has its own innocent explanation, revealed or inferable.
- Keep a clue plan in the story bible (`long-form-workflow.md`): what each character knows, and when.
- The reveal works through the detective's demonstration and the characters' reactions. Keep it brief, and don't follow it with paragraphs of recap (§7.8).
- **Bangladeshi procedural realism:** thana, OC, SI, জিডি, মামলা, ময়নাতদন্ত. Use the terms correctly, and go only as deep as you can keep accurate. An amateur sleuth needs a believable reason the police aren't solving the case.

## Thriller

- Make the stakes clear early: what will be lost, by whom, by when. Add a clock or a pressure that keeps tightening.
- The protagonist drives the plot through choices; things don't just happen to them. The antagonist is competent, because easy villains make cheap victories.
- Escalate in kind, not only in volume: new costs, reversals, allies who turn.
- Vary chapter endings (§7.7). Hooks are fine, but not the same cliffhanger every time. Quiet chapters give the next spike its force.
- Technical and procedural detail (weapons, hacking, police, medicine) is either accurate or kept vague. Don't invent specifics a knowledgeable reader would catch (§9).

## Horror

- Dread over gore. What's withheld frightens more than what's described. Build it from the ordinary going slightly wrong.
- The supernatural has rules, even if they are never explained. Break them only on purpose (§7.2, §15.2).
- Bengali folk horror is a deep tradition: নিশির ডাক, মেছো ভূত, শাঁকচুন্নি, পেতনি, আলেয়া, জিন, the haunted জমিদার বাড়ি. Use specific, regionally grounded lore with its own logic, not a decorative list of names. Don't mock or misstate the religious belief behind some lore, such as জিন in Islamic belief.
- Characters react the way people would: they lock doors, call someone, and have a reason to stay.
- Ambiguity at the end is allowed (§7.10). Inconsistency is not.

## Romance and romantic drama

- Both leads have lives, wants and flaws outside the relationship. Attraction grows from specific moments, not declarations.
- Obstacles come from values, circumstances and choices: family expectations, class, career, faith, distance. Portray them with nuance. A single misunderstanding that one honest conversation would fix can't carry a book (§15.1).
- Possessiveness, stalking and coercion are not romantic shorthand. If the story depicts them, it treats them as what they are.
- In Bengali romance, a shift in register (আপনি → তুমি) or a small gesture carries great weight. Use these as beats.
- Readers care about the ending type. If the brief doesn't say whether it's happy, bittersweet or tragic, ask. Bengali romantic drama accepts tragedy more readily than the romance-genre convention does.

## Adventure

- Physical geography and logistics are part of the pleasure. Describe real places (সুন্দরবন, বান্দরবান, সাজেক, সেন্ট মার্টিন, হাওর) accurately: tides, seasons, travel times, permits, plants and animals. Invent fictional places freely, but keep them consistent.
- Set pieces need a clear spatial layout so the reader can follow the action.
- Competence and preparation, or their absence, matter. Survival details are plausible.
- Indigenous communities, for example in the Chittagong Hill Tracts, are written specifically and respectfully, not as exotic backdrop (§13.3).

## Religious and spiritual writing

This is the category where fabrication does the most harm (§9.1, §14.3, §15.9).

- Never write a Quran verse, a hadith or a scripture passage from memory as a quotation unless you can verify it. Cite precisely:
  - Quran: the sura's name and the ayah number (সূরা আল-বাকারা, আয়াত ২৫৫).
  - Hadith: the collection and the hadith number, plus the authenticity grade (সহিহ, হাসান, যঈফ) when the source gives it.
- If you can't verify a text now, paraphrase it clearly as a paraphrase, with no quotation marks and no citation. Then add a verification note outside the manuscript. A fabricated hadith misleads readers about their faith; it is not a style problem.
- Say whose translation you quote, or that the rendering is your own.
- Where scholars or schools differ, say so. Don't present one view as the consensus (§15.9), and don't disparage the others.
- Apply honorifics consistently: (সা.) after the Prophet's name, (রা.) for companions, (আ.) for prophets, (রহ.) for scholars. Alternatively, use the full forms if the house prefers them.
- Hindu, Buddhist and Christian texts and traditions get the same accuracy and respect.
- In religious fiction, characters quote scripture only in verified wording. Never put an invented ruling in a scholar character's mouth.

## Children's and young adult

- Match vocabulary and sentence length to the age band (§15.6). Early readers get short sentences but real stories. Don't end with a moral lecture; let the story carry the value.
- Teenagers in YA sound like teenagers today, not like adults' memories of being one. Serious themes are allowed, handled with care (§13.4).
